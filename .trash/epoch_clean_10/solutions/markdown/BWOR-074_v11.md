## 1. Index Sets & Parameters

**Sets:**
- $G = \{A, B, C, D, E\}$: Set of grades, indexed by $g$.
- $P = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$: Set of sorting positions (steps), indexed by $p$.

**Parameters:**
- $N = 50,000$: Total number of participants (answer sheets).
- $r_g$: Percentage distribution of grade $g$.
  - $r_A = 0.10$
  - $r_B = 0.25$
  - $r_C = 0.30$
  - $r_D = 0.20$
  - $r_E = 0.15$
- $n_g$: Number of sheets for grade $g$, calculated as $n_g = N \times r_g$.
  - $n_A = 5,000$
  - $n_B = 12,500$
  - $n_C = 15,000$
  - $n_D = 10,000$
  - $n_E = 7,500$

## 2. Decision Variables

Let $x_{g,p}$ be a binary variable where:
$$
x_{g,p} = \begin{cases} 
1 & \text{if grade } g \text{ is scanned (sorted) at position } p \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$
for all $g \in G$ and $p \in P$.

## 3. Objective Function

The "workload" of scanning at position $p$ is proportional to the number of sheets remaining in the stack at that step.
- At position $p=1$, the machine scans all $N$ sheets.
- At position $p=2$, the machine scans all sheets *except* those of the grade identified in step 1. The count is $N - n_{g_1}$.
- At position $p$, the machine scans $N - \sum_{k=1}^{p-1} n_{g_k}$ sheets.

To minimize the total workload, we minimize the sum of the number of sheets scanned at each step.
Let $S_p$ be the number of sheets remaining at step $p$.
The total workload $W$ can be expressed in terms of the position of each grade.
If grade $g$ is placed at position $p_g$, it is removed from the stack for all subsequent steps $p > p_g$.
Equivalently, a sheet of grade $g$ is scanned at every step $p$ such that $p \le p_g$? No, that's not quite right.
Let's look at the cost contribution of each grade.
A sheet of grade $g$ is scanned during step 1, step 2, ..., step $p_g$ (the step where it is identified and removed). It is NOT scanned in steps $p_g + 1, \dots, 5$.
Wait, if it is identified at step $p_g$, it is removed *after* the scan of step $p_g$? Or is the scan the act of identification?
"Each scan can only detect one specific grade".
Step 1: Scan all 50,000. Detect Grade $g_1$. Remove/Sort them.
Step 2: Scan remaining $50,000 - n_{g_1}$. Detect Grade $g_2$. Remove/Sort them.
...
Step 5: Scan remaining $n_{g_5}$. Detect Grade $g_5$.

The total workload is:
$$ \text{Total Workload} = \sum_{p=1}^{5} \left( N - \sum_{k=1}^{p-1} n_{g_k} \right) $$
Expanding this:
$$ = N + (N - n_{g_1}) + (N - n_{g_1} - n_{g_2}) + (N - n_{g_1} - n_{g_2} - n_{g_3}) + (N - n_{g_1} - n_{g_2} - n_{g_3} - n_{g_4}) $$
Grouping by $n_g$:
- $n_{g_1}$ appears in terms $p=2,3,4,5$ (4 times).
- $n_{g_2}$ appears in terms $p=3,4,5$ (3 times).
- $n_{g_3}$ appears in terms $p=4,5$ (2 times).
- $n_{g_4}$ appears in term $p=5$ (1 time).
- $n_{g_5}$ appears in 0 terms (it is removed at step 5, so it is not in the "remaining" count for any subsequent step, and the step 5 count is just $n_{g_5}$, which is $N - \sum_{k=1}^4 n_{g_k}$. Wait.
Let's re-evaluate the sum:
$W = 5N - 4n_{g_1} - 3n_{g_2} - 2n_{g_3} - 1n_{g_4} - 0n_{g_5}$?
Let's check the last term.
Term $p=5$ is $N - n_{g_1} - n_{g_2} - n_{g_3} - n_{g_4} = n_{g_5}$.
So, $W = N + (N-n_{g_1}) + (N-n_{g_1}-n_{g_2}) + (N-n_{g_1}-n_{g_2}-n_{g_3}) + n_{g_5}$.
Substitute $n_{g_5} = N - \sum_{k=1}^4 n_{g_k}$:
$W = 5N - 4n_{g_1} - 3n_{g_2} - 2n_{g_3} - n_{g_4}$.
Notice that the constant $5N$ is fixed. To minimize $W$, we must minimize $- 4n_{g_1} - 3n_{g_2} - 2n_{g_3} - n_{g_4}$.
This is equivalent to **Maximizing** $4n_{g_1} + 3n_{g_2} + 2n_{g_3} + n_{g_4}$.
Alternatively, we can view the cost of assigning grade $g$ to position $p$ (where $p=1$ is first, $p=5$ is last).
The number of times a sheet of grade $g$ is scanned is equal to its position $p_g$.
- If $g$ is at $p=1$, it is scanned 1 time.
- If $g$ is at $p=2$, it is scanned 2 times (in step 1 and step 2).
- If $g$ is at $p=3$, it is scanned 3 times.
- If $g$ is at $p=4$, it is scanned 4 times.
- If $g$ is at $p=5$, it is scanned 5 times.
Total Workload $W = \sum_{g \in G} n_g \times p_g$.
We want to minimize $\sum_{g \in G} n_g \times p_g$.
This is a classic assignment problem (or rearrangement inequality). To minimize the sum of products of two sequences, one should sort one in ascending order and the other in descending order.
- $n_g$ values: $n_C=15,000$, $n_B=12,500$, $n_D=10,000$, $n_E=7,500$, $n_A=5,000$.
- Positions $p$: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$.
To minimize the sum, assign the largest $n_g$ to the smallest $p$, and the smallest $n_g$ to the largest $p$.
Order of $n_g$ descending: $C (15k), B (12.5k), D (10k), E (7.5k), A (5k)$.
Assign positions ascending: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$.
Mapping:
$p=1 \rightarrow C$
$p=2 \rightarrow B$
$p=3 \rightarrow D$
$p=4 \rightarrow E$
$p=5 \rightarrow A$

So the optimal order is **C, B, D, E, A**.

Let's formalize the LP/MILP model. Since this is a small assignment problem ($5 \times 5$), a binary integer program is appropriate and exact.

**Objective Function:**
Minimize
$$ Z = \sum_{g \in G} \sum_{p \in P} n_g \cdot p \cdot x_{g,p} $$

**Constraints:**
1. Each grade is assigned to exactly one position:
   $$ \sum_{p \in P} x_{g,p} = 1 \quad \forall g \in G $$
2. Each position is assigned to exactly one grade:
   $$ \sum_{g \in G} x_{g,p} = 1 \quad \forall p \in P $$
3. Binary constraints:
   $$ x_{g,p} \in \{0, 1\} \quad \forall g \in G, p \in P $$

## 4. Operational Constraints

The model is a standard Linear Assignment Problem.

**Verification of Solution:**
Using the Rearrangement Inequality logic derived above:
- Sort grades by count $n_g$ in **descending** order: $C(15,000), B(12,500), D(10,000), E(7,500), A(5,000)$.
- Assign to positions $p$ in **ascending** order: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$.
- Resulting Order:
  1. C
  2. B
  3. D
  4. E
  5. A

Let's calculate the workload for this order to ensure correctness.
$W = 15000(1) + 12500(2) + 10000(3) + 7500(4) + 5000(5)$
$W = 15,000 + 25,000 + 30,000 + 30,000 + 25,000 = 125,000$.

Let's check the alternative "Reverse Order" (smallest count first):
Order: A, E, D, B, C
$W = 5000(1) + 7500(2) + 10000(3) + 12500(4) + 15000(5)$
$W = 5,000 + 15,000 + 30,000 + 50,000 + 75,000 = 175,000$.
$125,000 < 175,000$, so the derived order is indeed better.

Let's check another random order, e.g., A, B, C, D, E (given order):
$W = 5000(1) + 12500(2) + 15000(3) + 10000(4) + 7500(5)$
$W = 5,000 + 25,000 + 45,000 + 40,000 + 37,500 = 152,500$.
$125,000 < 152,500$.

The mathematical model is:

**Sets:**
$G = \{A, B, C, D, E\}$
$P = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$

**Parameters:**
$n_A=5000, n_B=12500, n_C=15000, n_D=10000, n_E=7500$

**Variables:**
$x_{g,p} \in \{0,1\}$

**Objective:**
$\min \sum_{g \in G} \sum_{p \in P} n_g \cdot p \cdot x_{g,p}$

**Constraints:**
$\sum_{p=1}^5 x_{g,p} = 1, \forall g \in G$
$\sum_{g \in G} x_{g,p} = 1, \forall p \in P$

**Optimal Solution Order:**
1. Grade C
2. Grade B
3. Grade D
4. Grade E
5. Grade A
