### Model Type Declaration
**Model Type:** MILP (Mixed Integer Linear Programming)
**Note:** Although the problem structure resembles a graph theory problem (minimum cycle cover), it is naturally formulated as a set partitioning/covering problem. Since the number of routes is small and discrete, the variables are binary (integer). A standard LP solver with integer constraints (MILP) or a pure LP relaxation (if integrality is guaranteed by the matrix structure, which is not strictly guaranteed here without checking total unimodularity, so MILP is the safe declaration) is required.

    **Frequency Requirements:**
    The problem states "Sailings per Day". This implies that on average, or in a steady state cycle, the ship must complete these legs at the specified rates.
    Route 1: 3 times/day.
    Route 2: 2 times/day.
    Route 3: 1 time/day.
    Route 4: 1 time/day.
    
    This is a **Minimum Fleet Sizing** problem based on the **Longest Route Time** or **Cycle Time** analysis.
    Since the ships are identical and can perform any combination of legs, we need to form cycles of operations such that the frequency constraints are met.
    
    Let $x_{k \to l}$ be the number of times a ship transitions from leg $k$ to leg $l$ in a representative cycle.
    However, a simpler approach for this specific type of problem (discrete, small set) is to define the **Required Fleet Size** based on the **Total Workload** and **Availability**.
    
    Actually, the standard formulation for this "Fleet Sizing with Turnaround" problem is a **Set Covering** or **Set Partitioning** problem where we define "Ship Cycles" (patterns of operations) and select the minimum number of these cycles to cover the required daily frequencies.
    
    **Alternative Interpretation (Standard OR Textbook Approach):**
    Calculate the **Total Daily Workload** in ship-days and divide by **Ship Availability**? No, that's for continuous.
    
    Let's look at the **Cycle Time Constraint**.
    If a ship performs a sequence of legs $L_1, L_2, \dots, L_m$ and returns to $L_1$, the total time of the cycle is $T_{cycle} = \sum I_{k, next(k)}$.
    In one cycle of duration $T_{cycle}$ days, the ship performs:
    - $n_1$ sailings of Route 1
    - $n_2$ sailings of Route 2
    - $n_3$ sailings of Route 3
    - $n_4$ sailings of Route 4
    
    The frequency requirement is that per day, we need:
    - $3$ sailings of R1
    - $2$ sailings of R2
    - $1$ sailing of R3
    - $1$ sailing of R4
    
    So, if we use $N$ ships, and each ship operates a specific cycle pattern $p$ with frequency vector $\vec{v}_p = (v_{1,p}, v_{2,p}, v_{3,p}, v_{4,p})$ and cycle length $T_p$, the average daily output of one ship following pattern $p$ is $\vec{v}_p / T_p$.
    We need to find the minimum number of ships $M$ and their patterns such that the sum of outputs meets the demand.
    
    This is complex to formulate as a linear program without enumerating all possible cycles.
    
    **Simpler Formulation: Graph-based Flow / Assignment**
    Since the number of legs is small (4 types), we can model this as finding the minimum number of ships such that there exists a flow in the leg-transition graph that satisfies the node demands (frequencies).
    
    Let $x_{k,l}$ be the number of ships that follow the transition $k \to l$ in the steady state.
    Wait, $x_{k,l}$ should represent the **rate** of transitions per day?
    
    Let's define variables based on the **Daily Frequency**.
    Let $f_k$ be the required frequency of leg $k$ per day.
    $f_1 = 3$
    $f_2 = 2$
    $f_3 = 1$
    $f_4 = 1$
    
    In a steady state, the number of ships *entering* leg $k$ per day must equal the number of ships *leaving* leg $k$ per day, which must equal $f_k$.
    
    Let $y_{k,l}$ be the number of ships per day that transition from leg $k$ to leg $l$.
    Note: These are flow rates.
    
    **Constraints:**
    1.  **Flow Conservation at Leg Nodes:**
        For each leg $k$, the total flow entering $k$ must equal the total flow leaving $k$, and this must equal the required frequency $f_k$.
        $$ \sum_{j} y_{j,k} = f_k $$
        $$ \sum_{l} y_{k,l} = f_k $$
        (Note: $y_{k,k}$ represents a ship doing leg $k$ twice in a row, i.e., $k \to k$).
        
    2.  **Ship Count Calculation:**
        The number of ships required is the sum over all legs of the **Time a Ship is Busy with Leg $k$** plus the **Time it is in Transition**?
        Actually, the number of ships is equal to the sum of the **Occupation Times**.
        For each transition $k \to l$, the ship is occupied for time $I_{k,l}$ (Start of $k$ to Start of $l$).
        If $y_{k,l}$ ships make this transition per day, the total ship-days contributed by these transitions is $y_{k,l} \times I_{k,l}$?
        No.
        
        Let's look at it via **Little's Law** or **Workload**.
        Total Ship-Days required per day = $\sum_{k} (\text{Rate of Leg } k) \times (\text{Duration of Leg } k + \text{Port Time After Leg } k)$?
        This is tricky because port time is shared.
        
        Let's use the **Cycle Decomposition** approach.
        The set of transitions $y_{k,l}$ forms a flow on the graph of legs.
        Any feasible flow can be decomposed into a set of **Cycles**.
        Let $C$ be the set of all elementary cycles in the complete directed graph of 4 legs.
        Let $z_c$ be the number of ships (or cycles) of type $c$ we deploy.
        Actually, since $y_{k,l}$ are real numbers (rates), we can decompose the flow $y$ into cycles.
        The total number of ships $M$ is given by:
        $$ M = \sum_{c \in Cycles} z_c $$
        Where $z_c$ is the number of ships operating cycle $c$.
        
        However, we don't need to enumerate cycles if we use the **Total Occupation Time** formula.
        The total number of ships $M$ is equal to the total "ship-time" required per day.
        Each ship is always busy (either sailing or turning around).
        The time a ship spends on leg $k$ plus the subsequent turnaround to the next leg is $I_{k, next}$.
        
        Consider the flow $y_{k,l}$.
        The total number of ships $M$ is:
        $$ M = \sum_{k \in L} \sum_{l \in L} y_{k,l} \times \frac{I_{k,l}}{1 \text{ day?}} $$
        Wait, $y_{k,l}$ is "transitions per day".
        If a ship makes $y_{k,l}$ transitions per day, it occupies the ship for $y_{k,l} \times I_{k,l}$ days?
        No.
        
        Let's trace one ship.
        Ship $S$ performs leg $k$, then waits/travels for turnaround, then performs leg $l$.
        The time from Start($k$) to Start($l$) is $I_{k,l}$.
        In one day, the ship completes $1 / \text{CycleTime}$ cycles? No.
        
        Let's use the **Average Occupation** argument.
        Total Demand for Leg $k$ is $f_k$ sailings/day.
        Each sailing of Leg $k$ requires the ship to be occupied for the duration of the sailing $S_k$ PLUS the port time associated with it.
        Which port time?
        Usually, we assign the turnaround time to the leg it *follows* or *precedes*.
        Let's assign the turnaround time $\tau_{k,l}$ to the transition.
        
        The total "ship-days" required per day is the sum over all transitions of (Rate of Transition $\times$ Time per Transition).
        $$ M = \sum_{k \in L} \sum_{l \in L} y_{k,l} I_{k,l} $$
        
        Let's verify this.
        Suppose 1 ship does $A \to B \to A$ repeatedly.
        Cycle time $T = I_{A,B} + I_{B,A}$.
        Frequency of A per day = $1/T$.
        Frequency of B per day = $1/T$.
        $y_{A,B} = 1/T$, $y_{B,A} = 1/T$.
        Formula: $M = (1/T) I_{A,B} + (1/T) I_{B,A} = (I_{A,B} + I_{B,A}) / T = T / T = 1$.
        Correct.
        
        So the objective is to **Minimize** $M = \sum_{k,l} y_{k,l} I_{k,l}$
        Subject to flow conservation and frequency constraints.
        
        **Variables:**
        $y_{k,l} \ge 0$ : Number of ship transitions from leg $k$ to leg $l$ per day.
        
        **Constraints:**
        1.  **Outflow from Leg $k$ equals Frequency $f_k$**:
            $$ \sum_{l \in L} y_{k,l} = f_k, \quad \forall k \in L $$
        2.  **Inflow to Leg $k$ equals Frequency $f_k$**:
            $$ \sum_{l \in L} y_{l,k} = f_k, \quad \forall k \in L $$
            *(Note: Since $\sum f_k$ is the same on both sides, if outflow constraints are met, inflow is automatically met in a closed system, but we include both for clarity or just one set. Actually, $\sum_k f_k = \sum_{k,l} y_{k,l}$. So $\sum_l y_{k,l} = f_k$ implies total outflow = total inflow. The specific inflow to node $k$ must also be $f_k$ for steady state.)*
            
        3.  **Non-negativity**:
            $$ y_{k,l} \ge 0 $$

        **Objective:**
        Minimize $Z = \sum_{k \in L} \sum_{l \in L} y_{k,l} I_{k,l}$

        **Is $Z$ an integer?**
        The problem asks for the "minimum number of ships". Ships are discrete.
        The LP relaxation might give a fractional answer (e.g., 4.5 ships).
        However, in these specific "minimum fleet" problems with such small numbers, the LP optimum is often integer, or we can simply solve the LP and round up?
        No, we must ensure the solution is valid.
        Actually, the problem is a **Minimum Cost Flow** problem on a specific network (the leg transition graph) where the "cost" is the time $I_{k,l}$ and the "demand" is the frequency.
        Wait, the "cost" is time. We want to minimize total time?
        No, we want to minimize the number of ships.
        The number of ships is exactly the total weighted sum of flows.
        Since $y_{k,l}$ are flows, and the network is small, we can solve this LP.
        If the result is not an integer, we need to check if an integer solution exists.
        Given the structure (bipartite-like or general graph), the constraint matrix might not be totally unimodular, so the LP solution might be fractional.
        However, for $N=4$ nodes, we can often find the integer solution by inspection or by adding integrality constraints.
        
        Let's check the matrix structure.
        It's a circulation problem with supplies/demands.
        The constraint matrix is node-arc incidence, which is Totally Unimodular.
        Therefore, if the RHS ($f_k$) is integer, the LP solution will be **integer** (or half-integer? No, TU matrices with integer RHS yield integer vertices).
        **Crucial Point:** The Node-Arc incidence matrix of a directed graph is Totally Unimodular.
        The constraints are $\sum_{l} y_{k,l} = f_k$ and $\sum_{l} y_{l,k} = f_k$.
        This is a standard **Minimum Cost Flow** problem (or rather, Minimize $\sum c_{kl} y_{kl}$ subject to flow conservation).
        Since the cost coefficients $I_{k,l}$ are integers, and the demands $f_k$ are integers, and the matrix is TU, **the optimal basic feasible solution will have integer values for $y_{k,l}$**.
        
        Therefore, we can formulate this as a **Linear Program (LP)**. The solver will return an integer number of ships if we minimize the sum.
        Wait, $y_{k,l}$ represents the *rate* of transitions.
        If $y_{k,l}$ is integer, does that mean $y_{k,l}$ ships transition?
        Yes, "number of transitions per day".
        Since the flow conservation holds, the total number of ships $M = \sum y_{k,l} I_{k,l}$ will be an integer because $y$ and $I$ are integers.
        
        So, **Model Type: LP** (with implicit integer solution due to TU property).

### Formal Formulation

**Index Sets:**
*   $K = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$: Set of leg types.
    *   Leg 1: $E \to D$
    *   Leg 2: $B \to C$
    *   Leg 3: $A \to F$
    *   Leg 4: $D \to B$

**Parameters:**
*   $f_k$: Required frequency of leg $k$ (sailings per day).
    *   $f_1 = 3$
    *   $f_2 = 2$
    *   $f_3 = 1$
    *   $f_4 = 1$
*   $I_{k,l}$: Time interval (in days) from the start of leg $k$ to the start of leg $l$, if a ship performs leg $k$ immediately followed by leg $l$.
    *   Calculated as $I_{k,l} = S_k + 1 (\text{unload}) + t_{Dest(k), Origin(l)} + 1 (\text{load})$.
    *   Where $S_k$ is sailing time of leg $k$.
    *   $S_1 = 17, S_2 = 3, S_3 = 7, S_4 = 13$.
    *   $t_{i,j}$ from Table 3-22.

    **Matrix of $I_{k,l}$ (Rows: From Leg $k$, Cols: To Leg $l$):**

    | $k \setminus l$ | 1 ($E \to D$) | 2 ($B \to C$) | 3 ($A \to F$) | 4 ($D \to B$) |
    | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
    | **1 ($E \to D$)** | $17+17+2 = 36$ | $17+13+2 = 32$ | $17+14+2 = 33$ | $17+0+2 = 19$ |
    | **2 ($B \to C$)** | $3+5+2 = 10$ | $3+3+2 = 8$ | $3+2+2 = 7$ | $3+15+2 = 20$ |
    | **3 ($A \to F$)** | $7+3+2 = 12$ | $7+8+2 = 17$ | $7+7+2 = 16$ | $7+20+2 = 29$ |
    | **4 ($D \to B$)** | $13+8+2 = 23$ | $13+0+2 = 15$ | $13+1+2 = 16$ | $13+13+2 = 28$ |

    *Note: $t_{D,D}=0$ for leg 4 to leg 4? No, Leg 4 ends at B. Leg 4 starts at D. $t_{B,D}=13$. So $I_{4,4} = 13 (S_4) + 1 (unl) + 13 (B \to D) + 1 (load) = 28$. Correct.*
    *Leg 1 ends at D. Leg 1 starts at E. $t_{D,E}=17$. $I_{1,1} = 17+1+17+1 = 36$. Correct.*
    *Leg 2 ends at C. Leg 2 starts at B. $t_{C,B}=3$. $I_{2,2} = 3+1+3+1 = 8$. Correct.*
    *Leg 3 ends at F. Leg 3 starts at A. $t_{F,A}=7$. $I_{3,3} = 7+1+7+1 = 16$. Correct.*

**Decision Variables:**
*   $y_{k,l} \in \mathbb{R}^+$: The number of ship transitions per day from leg $k$ to leg $l$.
    *   Domain: Continuous non-negative. (Will be integer due to TU property).

**Objective Function:**
Minimize the total number of ships $M$:
$$ \text{Minimize } Z = \sum_{k=1}^{4} \sum_{l=1}^{4} I_{k,l} y_{k,l} $$

**Operational Constraints:**

1.  **Outflow Conservation (Frequency Satisfaction):**
    The total rate at which ships leave leg $k$ to perform any subsequent leg must equal the required frequency of leg $k$.
    $$ \sum_{l=1}^{4} y_{k,l} = f_k, \quad \forall k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $$
    Specifically:
    $$ \sum_{l=1}^{4} y_{1,l} = 3 $$
    $$ \sum_{l=1}^{4} y_{2,l} = 2 $$
    $$ \sum_{l=1}^{4} y_{3,l} = 1 $$
    $$ \sum_{l=1}^{4} y_{4,l} = 1 $$

2.  **Inflow Conservation (Steady State):**
    The total rate at which ships enter leg $k$ from any previous leg must equal the required frequency of leg $k$.
    $$ \sum_{k=1}^{4} y_{k,l} = f_l, \quad \forall l \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $$
    Specifically:
    $$ \sum_{k=1}^{4} y_{k,1} = 3 $$
    $$ \sum_{k=1}^{4} y_{k,2} = 2 $$
    $$ \sum_{k=1}^{4} y_{k,3} = 1 $$
    $$ \sum_{k=1}^{4} y_{k,4} = 1 $$

3.  **Non-negativity:**
    $$ y_{k,l} \ge 0, \quad \forall k, l \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $$
